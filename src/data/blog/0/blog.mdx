## How I graduated at the top of my class in Computer Science

##### <div className="kbd">31st October 2025</div>
##### *10min read*
---

Before reading this I recommend watching Veritasium's video on 
[Luck and Survivorship Bias](https://youtu.be/3LopI4YeC4I?si=hkJ65gI2iKdqr7lI).
What I will discuss worked because of who I am, my environment, luck and likely many factors I am not aware of. 
If you are a student, don't take this as gospel.

#### Choosing the right degree
I am a mediocre classic academic, at A-Level, I finished with a B in Physics and a C in Maths.
I chose Computer Science because it avoids my main weaknesses (writing, maths, theory)
and is coursework heavy, rewarding my strengths (practicality, problem solving and thirst for novelty in tech).

It is cliche advice. But I've seen people try to power through Computer Science when it's the wrong fit because
 it is any of: "Valuable", "High-Paying", "Respectable" degree. 

People argue, regardless, it is a matter of discipline and hard work to overcome. Yes you can, but should you?
 (not a rhetorical question)

Notice how infrequently I have to tap into willpower compared to Maths? I already consume a ton of engineering content on
YouTube, listen to engineering podcasts, love coding side projects and almost everything just clicks. The only times I
 needed to leverage willpower were the writing and maths bits of the degree.

Willpower is a limited resource that can be slow to recover and I do everything to minimise how often I'm forced 
to tap into it. If you are already treading water on willpower, and a storm comes - you will sink.
You will burn out and have no capacity to perform crunches when needed.

However both discipline and willpower can be trained, expanded and are important to master - but that is a different topic. If 
you are curious I recommend reading 
[Can't Hurt Me by David Goggins](https://books.google.nl/books?id=OSchEAAAQBAJ&lpg=PA1&pg=PA1#v=onepage&q&f=false). 
He became a top U.S. Navy SEAL operator while being genetically negatively buoyant and having a heart condition where his
 muscles receive half the oxygen needed. He is a master of discipline.

I am lucky to have Computer Science as a good match, I am not sure what I would do, had I been born 100 years ago.

#### Environment
Some students say they "go to the library or a cafe for work".

I work best at home, I've built an environment tailored for my workflow, comfort and to minimise strain.
- I've set up two monitors comfortably so I can see more with minimal eye movement and less neck strain.
- Standing desk
- Backlighting which minimises screen/background contrast eye strain
- Comfortable keyboard + mouse
- Dock + KVM to switch my peripherals and monitors to a work laptop with one click
- Dual boot Windows and Linux (more later)
- Smallest room with high window/room ratio (more later)

I don't see myself ever replicating this in a cafe. 

Also, a trip to a cafe as a precondition for work, creates an obstacle to start doing work. If you are curious about making 
good habits I recommend 
[Atomic Habits by James Clear](https://books.google.com/books/about/Atomic_Habits.html?id=fFCjDQAAQBAJ).

During COVID, psychologists recommended creating rituals for mental work/life divide at home, which could be just putting 
on a work shirt. My take on this is having dual boot partitions for Windows and Linux. When I am in downtime mode, I 
boot into Windows - it has my games installed. In work mode I boot into Linux - no games but has all my dev software.

#### Friends
I've seen students go into solitary or "monk mode", some uninstall WhatsApp for weeks to complete coursework.

I could not disagree more with this.

Going into solitary/ "monk mode" can work very short term, but on the discipline/ willpower graph, people force their 
intensity line to max out regardless of where the tipping point is. They incur fatigue and train the subconscious to start 
associating work with solitary. It can also do the inverse of creating an association of downtime with guilt and evidence 
for lack of productivity. 

Friends and family are not to be seen as a distraction at the expense of work. Psychologically they are key to unwinding, 
ordering thoughts and good friends will even point out blind spots, making work that much better.

Where cutting off can be reasonable, is if they are bad friends and their only idea of going out to catch up is getting
 wasted, a hangover will do more damage than good. Then it is better to be lonely and go on a search for new and more 
 like-minded friends.

Chris Williamson who has interviewed many top performers in various fields has made this same 
[observation](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/DF_FkTOAIXw).

#### Memes, Breaks and Downtime
People like to look down on memes - they are exactly what I need in my day. When
solving hard problems and so much of my life being serious and highly optimised, it is easy to 
become mentally stuck. Spending even just 1 min disconnecting from my challenges, in the opposite domain,
resets me to try to tackle a problem from a different angle. 

There is a running joke that engineers have 10 coffee/tea breaks a day. I usually do 1-2,
but they are also great for stepping away from output and making space for thinking about hard problems. In this domain 
tea/thinking time is also working time.

High-quality downtime is important, programming is mentally intense. One way I do this is I time-box my work day and
have Saturdays as sacred rest time and Sundays for church and family, I avoid blurring life/ work divide. Even though I 
might technically lose out on extra time spent on problems, the quality when I am back makes up for that and the proof
is in the pudding.

Being able to live with parents without needing a part time job was luck and a privilege, it was one less thing to 
drain my willpower bucket and left more capacity for high quality work. Universities like
 [Cambridge advise capping side work to 6-10hrs](https://www.cambridgestudents.cam.ac.uk/your-course/postgraduate-study/working-while-you-study).

#### Sleep
Learning the science of sleep has been useful. I recommend watching this video 
[The Science & Practice of Perfecting Your Sleep](https://youtu.be/JaRGJVrJBQ8?si=TzFZfRThCPM0M8vO). I am
far from perfection but understanding the following has improved clarity in my day: 
- you cannot catch up on sleep
- importance of light exposure within 15min of waking
- bedtime rituals + habits
- CONSISTENCY of sleeping + waking timing is as important as TIME sleeping

#### Health
I train in the gym, doing a mix of powerlifting and bodybuilding 6 days a week. I started at 120 kg to lose weight. Before 
I started my cortisol (stress hormone) blood pressure was through the roof. 
After losing weight my hormones normalised, my anxiety dropped, clarity and focus improved and on top of that I have a 
physique I'm comfortable in.

I will save the LinkedIn audience from progress photos, but if you're curious you can see them on my gym Instagram account

Caffeine is an awesome drug! (When used correctly.)
Caffeine half-life can vary anywhere from 1.5 to 9.5 hours, experimenting is the only way to find out. For me if I 
have a coffee at lunch it will boost my work and last into my gym session in the evening, but not so long it ruins my sleep.
This [Caffeine Simulator Website](https://observablehq.com/@elibryan/caffeine-simulator) is great for figuring out what a
sensible caffeine amount and timing looks like for you.

I also do 1-week caffeine breaks for every 3 weeks of caffeine use to prevent tolerance.

Motivation is a weak force to be leveraged but not depended upon.

It is much better to rely on discipline, the ability to do something regardless of how I feel. The backwards arrow is 
inconsistent, some days it might be small, other days it might be 20x the size.

But if motivation works for me it is a bonus and there are ways to maximise the arrows pointing forward.

There is a famous proverb, "Hope for the best, prepare for the worst". Many say it, few live it.

"Hoping for the best" is the positive vision, xyz is going to be the result of this - the more detailed, the better. It must 
be honest, you'd be surprised how many top performers say their motivation is wealth, status or women. Some say this is 
shallow, but it is honest and these people leveraged that force to overcome great challenges.
Maybe you are a wholesome human being and it really is solely creating positive change for the world but be real and 
honest to maximise this. For me it's novelty, I want to be at the tip of the spear to have the privilege to work on the 
most novel projects.

The pushing arrow is fear, anxiety, "preparing for the worst". For me this fear was very real and much 
larger than the pull. Computer Science is a field with one of the highest unemployment rates in 2025, a degree is not
enough and there is nothing I would hate more than being stuck in a basic job like retail, living paycheck to paycheck 
and no future development prospects. I have a love for engineering and it would be wasted in this scenario.

I am a neurotic person, I've learned while anxiety is a negative emotion, it is not a negative force when 
leveraged correctly.

The two visions, detailed and grounded in reality, work together to create a strong force that drives me forward.

#### Organisation & Strategy
Leveraging all previous gains and having a lot of extra water in my willpower bucket I applied it to organisation and
strategy.

I had smaller visions but associated with actionable strategy, for modules with multiple submissions I told myself two stories:
- Let's get a grade so high on this checkpoint so the next one will be easy and I can achieve a 1st with minimal effort.
- If I get ill and cannot submit later, let's get a grade high enough so I still pass the module.

I tracked all assessments on a spreadsheet and artificially inserted bad future grades to project hard times. This made me 
perform in the present to succeed regardless. When I got to my final project my average was high enough to graduate 1st-class
even with a passing grade in my dissertation.

I always rationed my work into small daily pieces and submitted a week early or at the latest 3 days early. 
This minimised the need for willpower by avoiding last-second crunches. It also gave me time to chat with classmates who 
were doing it last-second and panicking and to correct my own work if I realised I misunderstood a requirement.

For courseworks where I could choose what I would do, I always picked something: fun, practical, novel and I could discuss in
an interview. This set my discipline/willpower tipping point higher. For my dissertation I went all out: I picked Rust 
(an untouched language), a Raspberry Pi as target platform (an embedded system I had no experience with) and a dashcam 
system as the project as I wanted to try out FFmpeg, apply my recent computer vision learnings and make something practical 
for my mum to use in her car. This ended up being one of my best pieces of work.

#### Prioritisation
I've learned on my placement year that wise time-cost/result analysis is key to maximising business-relevant delivery. 

In university this can be even easier with mark schemes spelling things out for you. If a mark scheme awards 10 marks for a
responsive frontend and 10 for CI/CD, I'll chose CI/CD first. It rewards better for how little time it requires.

A criteria like "pretty" can also be subjective and relative to the marks awarded or the standard of your peers. There may
not be a need to perfect responsiveness if you know it will only run on a 1080p PC monitor or get pixel perfect spacing 
consistency.

#### Leadership & Team Work
The first uni team project broke me and many others morally, teamwork isn't natural to most tech geeks but luckily it can be
learned. I won't go into detail but there were many learnings. 

Watching just a few videos on YouTube on how to lead software development teams, being a good teammate, creating healthy 
team dynamics and navigating difficult situations can work wonders. I recommend 
[Healthy Developer on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/@HealthyDev). 

For my second team project, and studying Teamwork and Dev Leadership, I was able to confidently take point - we scored 98% 
for that module, no one was burnt out, everyone had autonomy, clear purpose and
 even extra time to code bonus features for fun. 

#### Conclusion and Luck
You have probably gathered some of these things might not be feasible for you to apply and your circumstances might be more
or less lucky. I have had the same conclusion from observing many other top performers too. The key is knowing oneself and 
experimenting to find what works rather than copying a blueprint. 

Managing the discipline/willpower tipping line and treating willpower like gold prevented me from burnout while performing. 

In fact it was not my plan to graduate at the top of my class, when I received the prize I was surprised. 

My main plan was to minimise my chance of not getting a job. But all of these things that people say they don't have time for: 
downtime, friends, gym, weekends, health, sleep, nutrition were not at the expense of my work but supported it - and drove
 me to the top.

Hopefully you found something useful from this, if you see me in person tell me what works for you and what differs :)
